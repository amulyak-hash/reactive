## 1.1 The Physical Transformation

Port Talbot is replacing its entire steelmaking process. Two blast furnaces (traditional route: iron ore + coke → molten iron at ~2,000°C → refined in Basic Oxygen Steelmaking converter → liquid steel) are being decommissioned. The traditional route required iron ore mining, coking coal, sinter plants, coke ovens, and blast furnaces running 24/7 for years without stopping.

All of that is shut down and being demolished. The last blast furnace closed September 2024. Over 2,500 workers have left. Coke ovens, sinter plant, BOS converters, harbour cranes — all being decommissioned. The Port Talbot skyline is physically changing.

Replacing it: a single Electric Arc Furnace. Instead of cooking iron ore with coke, the EAF melts scrap steel using three massive graphite electrodes that create electric arcs at temperatures up to 1,800°C. Scrap melts, impurities are removed with oxygen and fluxes (lime, dolomite), alloys are added, and the liquid steel is tapped into a ladle for further refining before going to the continuous caster.

This is not a like-for-like swap. It is a fundamentally different process — different raw materials, different energy source, different workforce skills, different supply chain.

### Project numbers

- £1.25 billion total investment (£500M from UK government)
- Construction broke ground July 14, 2025
- Commissioning target: end of 2027
- Production capacity: 3 million tonnes/year
- CO₂ reduction: ~90%, equivalent to 5 million tonnes/year (1.5% of total UK emissions)
- Jobs: 5,000 secured (2,800 lost from blast furnace closure)

### Construction status (April 2026)

- Over 360,000 tonnes of material excavated from site
- 1,000+ piles being driven up to 28 meters deep for foundations
- Fume extract plant and Consteel conveyor structure foundations in progress
- Legacy buildings demolished (old substation, compressor house, mechanical workshop, annealing bays)
- Lagoon partially filled to create a scrap transport road to the melt shop
- Harbour returning to ABP management, cranes dismantled
- Old BOS building being gutted — EAF will be installed in its south-west corner
- New transfer tracks being planned to link EAF → ladle furnaces → continuous caster

### Live supply chain issue

Critical EAF shell components are manufactured in China — the only location globally capable of fabricating certain pieces. Shipping rerouted from Suez Canal to Cape of Good Hope due to Red Sea security risks (Houthi attacks, piracy, regional instability). This added weeks to transit and increased freight costs. Overall timeline unaffected because parallel construction phases absorb the delay.

## 1.2 The Specific Technology — Tenova Consteel® EAF

This is not a generic EAF. Tata contracted Tenova (Italy) to supply a specific system.

### Conventional EAF vs. Consteel®

Conventional: scrap loaded into buckets, roof opens, buckets tip scrap in, roof closes, electrodes lower, arc melts scrap. Batch process. Each roof-open cycle loses heat and creates fugitive emissions.

Consteel®: scrap loaded onto an enclosed conveyor from the scrap yard. Hot exhaust gas from the EAF flows counter-current over the scrap, preheating it. Preheated scrap feeds continuously into the furnace and melts by immersion in the existing liquid steel bath. The roof never opens during charging.

Implications: continuous feeding means more stable arc and power draw, less grid flicker. Preheating with waste gas = energy recovery = lower electricity per tonne. No roof-open cycle = fewer fugitive emissions. Can handle mixed charge: 100% scrap, 100% DRI/HBI, or any combination.

### Electromagnetic Stirring (Consteerrer®)

ABB's ArcSave® technology. An electromagnetic stirrer built into the furnace bottom stirs the liquid bath without physical contact. Improves thermal homogeneity, accelerates melting, improves steel quality, reduces energy consumption. Exclusive Tenova-ABB joint technology.

### Capacity

450 tonnes/hour of liquid steel. Tapping rate: 320 tonnes every 42 minutes. Among the largest EAFs in the world.

### Full Tenova supply scope

- Consteel® EAF with continuous charging system
- Electromagnetic stirring system (EMS/Consteerrer®)
- Two ladle furnaces (same capacity class)
- Fume treatment plant
- Material handling system
- Engineering and assistance package through construction and startup

### Confirmed technology partners

- Tenova (Italy) — EAF, ladle furnaces, fume treatment, material handling, commissioning assistance
- ABB (Switzerland) — electromagnetic stirring, power systems
- Clecim (France) — downstream processing equipment
- Sir Robert McAlpine — main construction contractor
- Regional contractors: Darlow Lloyd & Sons, Mii, Skelton Thomas, Wernick Buildings, Andrew Scott Ltd, Systems Group

### The heat cycle

1. Charging: scrap continuously feeds via Consteel® conveyor, preheated by off-gas
2. Melting: electric arcs from three electrodes melt scrap by immersion in liquid bath. Electromagnetic stirring homogenizes temperature
3. Refining: oxygen injection removes carbon and other elements. Carbon injection creates foamy slag (insulates arc, improves energy efficiency). Flux additions condition slag
4. Superheating: final temperature adjustment to ~1,600°C tapping temperature
5. Tapping: furnace tilts, liquid steel flows through taphole into preheated ladle. Alloy additions during tapping. Some liquid steel and slag retained as "hot heel" to accelerate next heat
6. Ladle furnace: temperature trimming, final alloy adjustment, argon stirring for homogenization, desulphurization
7. Continuous caster: liquid steel from ladle poured into tundish, then into water-cooled copper mold, solidifying into slabs for rolling

Each step has consumable inputs (electrodes wearing, refractories eroding, fluxes consumed, oxygen used, alloys added) and measurable outputs (energy per heat, tap-to-tap time, temperature profile, chemistry profile, slag composition).

## 1.3 The Procurement Universe

The organization purchases both products (discrete items with specs and quantities) and services (labor and expertise charged per hour, per day, or as time and material). These have completely different ordering behaviors, decision patterns, and evaluation criteria. The experience center cannot treat them the same.

### PRODUCTS — Things with specs, quantities, and unit prices

**Heavy Mechanical Equipment**

- EAF shell (partially manufactured in China — only capable source globally)
- Electrode arms and masts (three per furnace, hydraulic positioning)
- Electrode clamping system (spring washer force, hydraulically released for slipping)
- Furnace tilting mechanism (for tapping steel and deslagging)
- Retractable roof and roof swing mechanism
- Water cooling panels (sidewall protection)
- Consteel® conveyor structure and enclosed housing
- Ladle furnace vessels (x2)
- Overhead cranes (ladle transport within melt shop)
- Transfer track systems (EAF → ladle furnaces → caster)
- Scrap yard equipment (cranes, magnets, shredders, sorting systems)
- Charging systems for additives

**Electrical Systems**

- EAF transformer(s): custom-designed, 150-200+ MVA, converts grid high voltage (10-110kV) to low voltage/high current (100-1200V). Extreme operating conditions — constant overcurrents, overvoltages from short circuits. On Load Tap Changer (OLTC). Lead time: 12-18+ months. Suppliers: ABB, Siemens, GE Vernova, SEA Trasformatori, Specialtrasfo
- Ladle furnace transformers (x2): smaller, similar design, currents up to 80kA+ through water-cooled tubular elbows
- Series reactors: improve arc stability, power factor, EAF efficiency
- Power quality/flicker mitigation: SVCs or STATCOMs — EAFs cause voltage flicker on the grid, utility has strict limits
- High-voltage switchgear and circuit breakers rated for furnace duty
- Bus bar systems: massive copper/aluminum conductors, potentially water-cooled
- Digital electrode regulation system (Tenova — real-time arc length optimization)
- Level 2 automation system (supervises complete melt shop operation)
- Grid connection/substation upgrades

**Refractory Materials (ongoing consumable — not one-time)**

- Furnace sidewalls: Magnesia-Carbon (MgO-C) bricks — 95% to 98% MgO grades. Resist arc temperature erosion, slag corrosion, oxy-fuel burner oxidation
- Furnace hearth/bottom: high-density magnesia or alumina bricks, supporting 300+ tonnes liquid steel
- Furnace roof: refractory-lined or water-cooled (most modern large EAFs use water-cooled roofs), roof centre as multi-piece precast items
- Taphole area: extremely high wear zone, specialized shapes, frequent replacement (sometimes every few heats)
- Ladle working lining: inner refractory contacting steel/slag directly
- Ladle permanent lining: backup behind working lining
- Slag line refractories: most aggressive chemical attack zone in ladle
- Castable refractories: gunning/shotcreting for faster re-lining
- Campaign life: 400-1200 heats per reline depending on zone and practice
- Major suppliers: Vesuvius (Supermag® range), RHI Magnesita (Austria), Krosaki Harima (Japan), Shinagawa Refractories (Japan), Imerys (France), Saint-Gobain SEFPRO
- Market context: global refractory market ~$32B in 2025, iron & steel = 67% of consumption. Demand for EAF-specific formulations surging as BF-BOF declines

**Graphite Electrodes (ongoing consumable)**

- Three electrodes per AC furnace, round section, threaded segments
- Wear down during operation — new segments added continuously
- Volatile pricing (2017-18 saw 300-400% spikes from Chinese needle coke crackdowns)
- Consumption depends on power-on time, oxygen practice, scrap quality
- Breakage is production-stopping
- Suppliers: GrafTech, Showa Denko, Tokai Carbon, HEG

**Scrap Steel Feedstock (most critical dynamic category)**

- Port Talbot needs ~2M tonnes/year from 2027
- UK generates 8-11M tonnes/year, exports ~80%
- Only ~2.6M tonnes currently recycled domestically
- Tata targeting 75% UK-sourced scrap
- Quality grades: HMS I (heavy melting scrap, >6mm thick, no galvanized), HMS II (lighter gauge), shredded (auto bodies/white goods), plate and structural (higher quality, predictable chemistry), rails (very clean, known chemistry), borings and turnings (machine shop waste, contamination risk)
- The contamination problem: copper and tin are "tramp elements" — once in steel, cannot be removed by any standard refining process. Automotive-grade flat steel (Port Talbot's target market) needs Cu <0.10%, Sn <0.015%. Contaminated scrap produces steel that cannot be sold to highest-value customers
- New hazard: lithium-ion batteries from end-of-life EVs entering scrap stream — fire risks, handling protocols not yet mature
- The structural problem: UK recyclers have had little incentive to invest in advanced shredding/screening technology because the domestic EAF market was tiny. Export markets accept dirtier scrap. Now demand is surging but infrastructure isn't ready

**Fluxes and Additives**

- Burnt lime (CaO): primary slag former
- Dolomite/magnesite (MgO): slag conditioning, refractory protection
- Carbon (coke/coal/anthracite): injected for slag foaming and chemistry adjustment
- Ferro-alloys: ferro-manganese, ferro-silicon, ferro-chrome added during tapping or in ladle furnace
- Oxygen: injected via lances for decarburization and scrap cutting

**Environmental/Emissions Equipment**

- Fume treatment plant (Tenova scope, already under construction)
- Primary fume extraction: direct off-gas capture through 4th hole in furnace roof, temperatures exceeding 1,000°C
- Secondary fume extraction: canopy/hood system capturing fugitive emissions during charging and tapping
- Gas cooling system: water-cooled, bringing gas below 200°C before filtration
- Baghouse dust collectors: pulse-jet fabric filters using high-temperature media (Nomex/aramid fibers), >99% particulate capture
- Electric Arc Furnace Dust (EAFD): 10-20 kg per tonne of steel = 30,000-60,000 tonnes/year. Contains zinc (20-25%), iron (30%), lead, cadmium, chromium. Classified hazardous waste. Recyclable via Waelz kiln (~$160-180/tonne processing)
- New 1.6M tonne pickle line with acid recovery and waste water collection — zero waste water/landfill target, reduces HGV movements

**Instrumentation & Controls**

- PLCs for furnace operations
- Tenova digital electrode regulation (real-time arc optimization)
- Multipoint oxygen/carbon injection control
- Temperature measurement (immersion thermocouples, optical pyrometers)
- Off-gas analysis (Goodfellow EFSOP® type — real-time CO, CO₂, O₂)
- Slag detection systems for tapping
- Scrap Tracking System (raw material input monitoring)
- SCADA for centralized monitoring
- MES integration (production scheduling, quality management, maintenance)
- Level 2 dynamic process control (closed-loop optimization)
- Cybersecurity (critical national infrastructure designation)

### How products get evaluated

Price, quality/grade, lead time, delivery reliability, past performance history, technical compliance with specs, vendor financial stability, origin/traceability (increasingly important with CBAM), safety and environmental certifications.

## 1.4 The Regulatory & Policy Landscape

### UK Steel Strategy (March 2026)

- £2.5B committed to rebuild and modernize UK steel
- Steel import quotas cut by 60% from July 1, 2026
- Out-of-quota tariff rate doubled to 50%
- Government exploring melt-and-pour origin requirements for steel imports
- Cross-government scrap working group forming by May 2026
- Voluntary scrap quality standards targeted by end-2026
- Mandatory national scrap standards by end-2027
- Digital scrap traceability systems by end-2028
- Hydrogen-DRI acknowledged as long-term route but investment decision deferred

### Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism

- UK CBAM launches January 2027
- Steel imports taxed on embedded carbon content
- EU CBAM already in effect — UK exports to EU face charges until UK-EU ETS linking complete
- Port Talbot's 90% CO₂ reduction becomes a competitive advantage, not just environmental

### Environmental Compliance

- EAFD classified as hazardous waste — strict handling, transport, disposal/recycling
- Continuous emissions monitoring obligations
- Fume treatment must achieve >98% capture of primary and secondary emissions
- Planning permission granted February 2025 with environmental conditions
- New pickle line zero-waste-water and zero-landfill targets

### Employment & Community

- 5,000 jobs commitment tied to government funding conditions
- Transition Board providing £100M for displaced worker and community support
- Trade union consultation requirements
- Local content expectations (not legally mandated but politically required)

## 2.1 Persona 1: Chief Project Director

**Archetype**: Strategic Decision Maker

Most senior person accountable for the entire £1.25B transformation. Reports to Tata Steel UK CEO and Tata Group Chairman. Carries political weight of the UK government's £500M investment and 5,000 jobs commitment.

### Decision cadence

- Weekly: Project steering meetings — top 5 risks, milestone status, cross-package dependencies
- Monthly: Executive reviews — budget variance, timeline health, political commitments
- Quarterly: Board updates — Tata Group and UK government reporting
- Ad-hoc: Escalations when a single decision could shift timeline or budget by weeks or millions

### Information needs

- High-altitude view — not individual POs or vendor quotes
- Aggregated risk heat maps ranked by timeline/budget impact
- Milestone trackers with variance and trend
- Budget variance summaries with forward projections
- Cross-package dependency alerts
- Drill-down capability in 2 clicks from any flagged issue

### Trust signals

- Track record of accurate predictions — if the system flagged a risk 3 months ago and it materialized, belief compounds
- Structured narrative over raw data — wants a story with numbers backing it, not a spreadsheet
- Consensus of trusted inner circle — if Head of Procurement, Head of Engineering, and CFO all agree, approves fast
- If system cried wolf repeatedly, stops looking entirely

### Behavioral patterns

- Time-starved, attention-scarce — gives the system 60 seconds to prove value on any visit
- Delegates investigation — needs "share this view" and "assign this alert" workflows
- Risk-averse on timeline — will spend more money to protect schedule (expedite shipping, dual-source critical items)
- Politically aware — every decision evaluated for optics with government, unions, media
- Relies on trusted inner circle (Head of Procurement, Head of Engineering, CFO) for filtered information

### Buying center role

Decision Maker — signs off on major vendor selections, budget reallocations, scope changes. Rarely initiates procurement — responds to escalations.

### Influence chain

Head of Procurement recommends → Head of Engineering validates technical fit → CFO confirms financial impact → Chief Project Director approves.

### Pain points

- No single view of cross-package risk — assembles from multiple reports and meetings
- Time spent in status meetings the system could replace
- Information consistently asked for that takes too long to produce
- Surprises that hit — disruptions the system should have caught

### Moments

- Clarity: "One screen shows project health — timeline, budget, top risks, what needs attention this week"
- Decision: "System recommended expediting transformer shipment at £200K vs. accepting 3-week delay. Showed downstream commissioning impact. Clear choice."
- Happiness: "Six months in, no surprises. System flagged three risks validated with team. None became problems because we acted early."

### Stress behavior

Under timeline pressure, defaults to spending more to protect schedule. Under political pressure, slows down to consult more stakeholders.

## 2.2 Persona 2: Head of Procurement

**Archetype**: Analytical Operator (Senior)

The PRIMARY daily user. Lives in the procurement function, managing vendor relationships across all active packages. Sits between technical teams (who define what's needed) and commercial teams (who manage contracts and payments).

### Decision cadence

- Daily: Approve POs, resolve delivery issues, handle vendor escalations, review incoming quality reports
- Weekly: Strategic reviews — sourcing strategy, spend analysis, risk assessment, contract renewals pipeline
- Monthly: Vendor performance reviews, budget reconciliation, market intelligence synthesis
- Quarterly: Sourcing strategy refresh, framework agreement renewals, supplier rationalization
- Ad-hoc: Emergency procurement when supply disruptions hit — speed trumps optimization

### Information needs

- Dense, multi-dimensional — price, quality, delivery, risk simultaneously for any vendor or category
- Historical vendor performance data to inform forward decisions
- Market intelligence — scrap prices by grade, refractory availability, electrode pricing trends
- Alerts that are specific and actionable, not generic
- Contract expiration calendar and reorder triggers
- Spend tracking vs. budget by category with drill-down
- Supply chain risk register — single sources, long lead times, geopolitical exposure

### Trust signals

- Data accuracy and completeness — if system shows vendor reliability at 94% but their experience says 85%, trust destroyed instantly
- Pattern surfacing — "Vendor X delivery drops every Q4, correlates with maintenance shutdown" builds trust fast
- Correctability — system that can't be argued with can't be trusted. Must accept overrides and learn
- Consistency over time — one good prediction doesn't build trust, six months of accurate data does

### Behavioral patterns

- Relationship-driven — personal relationships with key vendor account managers built over years. Will defend known vendor against data unless evidence is overwhelming
- Pattern-dependent — established routines: checks certain reports Monday AM, reviews POs Tuesday PM, vendor calls Wednesday. System must fit existing rhythms, not demand new ones
- Risk-calibrated by category — conservative on critical path items, more experimental on non-critical/long-lead
- Spreadsheet-native — currently manages much of work in Excel. System must be at least as fast and flexible or they'll revert
- Under constant pressure from two directions — engineering wants best technical solution regardless of cost, finance wants cheapest regardless of risk. Lives in the tension between them

### Buying center role

Recommender and Gatekeeper — shapes shortlist, runs evaluation, presents recommendation. Doesn't always have final sign-off (depends on value threshold) but recommendation rarely overridden.

### Influence chain

Engineering defines technical requirements → Head of Procurement sources options, evaluates, recommends → CFO approves above threshold → Chief Project Director approves above higher threshold.

### Pain points

- Fragmented information — multiple systems, spreadsheets, email threads for a single vendor decision
- Reactive firefighting vs. proactive strategic sourcing — too much time on delivery delays and quality issues
- Decisions made with insufficient information — especially market intelligence on scrap and refractories
- Vendor performance tracked manually or not at all over time
- Approval workflow friction between recommendation and decision

### Moments

- Clarity: "See state of all active packages in 30 seconds. Three on track, one flagged, one needs decision today."
- Decision: "System compared four refractory vendors across price, campaign life, delivery reliability, slag chemistry compatibility. Vendor B clearly best. Can see why."
- Happiness: "System flagged scrap supplier quality drift two weeks before it would have caused a production issue. Switched in time. Saved a campaign."

### Stress behavior

Under time pressure, defaults to known vendor — calls contact directly, bypasses system. Under budget pressure, shifts from "best value" to "lowest cost" unless engineering justifies the premium.

- Government milestone compliance — conditions attached to £500M grant
- Simple, human-readable metrics backed by verifiable data
- Talking points and narratives, not technical detail

### Trust signals

- Consistency between external communications and actual performance — if they tell Welsh Government local content is 60% and actual is 45%, trust with government destroyed and potentially the £500M funding relationship jeopardized
- Data validated by multiple internal teams before being shared externally
- Numbers that can withstand media scrutiny and parliamentary questions
- Timeliness — stale data in a live press situation is worse than no data

### Behavioral patterns

- Narrative-first — thinks in stories, not data. "200 construction jobs in Neath Port Talbot this quarter" is more useful than a spend-by-region pie chart
- Relationship-dependent — effectiveness comes from personal relationships with ministers, council leaders, union officials
- Reactive to external events — media article, union complaint, minister's question triggers urgent data requests needing fast answers
- Risk-sensitive about optics — will flag if procurement decision perceived negatively (Chinese supplier over Welsh for visible component)
- Long-term perspective — thinks about the relationship over decades, not individual transactions

### Buying center role

Influencer on Non-Commercial Criteria — pushes for local content, community benefit, political alignment. Can escalate to Chief Project Director if procurement decision threatens government relationship.

### Influence chain

Government Relations Lead flags political/community implications → Chief Project Director weighs against commercial/technical factors → may override pure commercial logic for strategic relationship reasons.

### Pain points

- Local content and employment data takes too long to produce when a minister calls
- No single source of truth for community impact metrics — assembles from multiple departments
- Transition Board's £100M spend and outcomes poorly tracked
- Data they wish they had for government reporting they currently can't get fast enough

### Moments

- Clarity: "Minister asks about local jobs. Answer in 10 seconds: 1,200 construction workers on-site, 68% from Wales."
- Decision: "Media asking about Chinese component shipment. System shows: which components, why China (only capable manufacturer), % of total value, UK local content overall."
- Happiness: "Transition Board review went perfectly. Every metric — jobs, local spend, community benefits — current and defensible."

### Stress behavior

Under media pressure, needs instant access to defensible facts. Under political pressure, prioritizes narrative control over procurement optimization.

## 2.7 Cross-Persona Dynamics

### Buying center scenarios

**Routine reprocurement** (refractories, electrodes, scrap — regular orders from established suppliers):
User: Head of Procurement. Influencer: Head of Engineering. Approver: HoP within threshold / CFO above. Other personas rarely involved.

**New vendor qualification** (first-time supplier for any category):
User: Head of Procurement. Influencer: Head of Engineering + ESG Lead. Approver: CFO + Chief Project Director if critical path. Government Relations involved if local content implications.

**Major capital procurement** (EAF components, transformers, large construction packages):
User: HoP (commercial) + HoE (technical). Influencer: all personas to varying degrees. Approver: CPD + CFO jointly. Government Relations active in communications.

**Emergency procurement** (supply disruption, equipment failure, quality rejection):
User: whoever identifies the problem. Decision maker: HoP or HoE, verbal approval formalized after. Defaults to known supplier. Speed trumps optimization. Pre-loaded alternatives critical.

### Trust-behavior relationship across personas

- Head of Procurement: trusts data volume and consistency. Show 12 months of delivery reliability scores.
- Head of Engineering: trusts technical evidence from comparable installations. "In use at 12 comparable EAFs" is persuasive.
- CFO: trusts financial modeling with clear assumptions. Stress-tests assumptions, approves if they hold.
- Chief Project Director: trusts consensus of trusted circle. All three agree = fast approval. Disagreement = defer.
- ESG Lead: trusts third-party certifications and audit trails. Self-reported vendor data insufficient.
- Government Relations Lead: trusts whatever can be publicly defended. "Can I say this to a minister?" is the test.

### Product vs. service behavioral split

Product procurement is data-dense and comparative. The cognitive work is analytical — pattern matching across multiple dimensions. Typical cycle: receive requirement → check frameworks → RFQ if needed → compare → evaluate → recommend → approve → PO. About 70-80% of recurring orders are repeat purchases from incumbents.

Service procurement is judgment-heavy and relationship-driven. You can't spec a commissioning engineer's capability the same way you spec a refractory brick. Evaluation is qualitative — reading references, assessing team CVs, judging whether past experience is truly comparable. The biggest judgment calls happen when two contractors both claim relevant experience but differ enormously in scale.

The experience center must handle both — with different UI, different data, different trust signals for each.

### Behavior under stress

Under time pressure: all personas default to the familiar. Known vendors, known products, known contractors. Decision quality degrades. The experience center must be fast in these moments — pre-loaded alternatives, one-click comparisons, cached recommendations.

Under budget pressure: CFO becomes more involved in individual decisions. HoP shifts from "best value" to "lowest cost." Risk tolerance drops. The experience center must surface cost avoidance opportunities proactively, before the crisis.

Under political pressure: Government Relations Lead gains outsized influence. Local content becomes a weighted criterion. Decisions slow as more stakeholders consult. The experience center must generate local content reports instantly.

Under supply disruption: emergency mode. Speed is everything. Existing alternatives selected instantly if pre-qualified. Non-qualified face extreme skepticism. The proactive alert system is tested — did it predict this? If yes, trust skyrockets. If missed, trust takes a hit.

## 3.2 Framework 2: Domain Expertise

The knowledge the system must have to generate credible insights, diagnoses, and prescriptions.

### Material Science

**Refractory zone selection**: Sidewalls need MgO-C bricks (95-98% MgO) to resist arc radiation, slag splash, oxy-fuel flame. Hearth needs high-density magnesia/alumina to support 300+ tonnes liquid steel. Taphole is extreme wear zone — specialized shapes, frequent replacement. Ladle slag line faces most aggressive chemical attack.

**Slag chemistry ↔ refractory wear**: slag composition (CaO, MgO, FeO, SiO₂, Al₂O₃) directly affects wear rates. High FeO attacks MgO-C aggressively. System must correlate slag practice changes with refractory performance changes automatically.

**Scrap chemistry constraints**: copper and tin cannot be removed once in steel. Automotive-grade flat steel needs Cu <0.10%, Sn <0.015%. System must track residual elements per supplier per grade.

**Electrode science**: graphite electrodes carry tens of thousands of amps. Consumption depends on power-on time, oxygen practice, scrap quality. Breakage is production-stopping. Quality varies by manufacturer — needle coke quality, impregnation, physical density.

### Process Engineering

**Energy efficiency relationships**: scrap preheating (waste heat recovery) ↔ foamy slag (arc insulation) ↔ electromagnetic stirring (heat transfer) ↔ off-gas post-combustion (chemical energy recovery). All interdependent. Changing one affects others. System must model these relationships.

**Consumable input → output correlation**: each heat step has measurable consumable inputs and outputs. System correlates inputs to outputs to identify optimization opportunities.

### Supply Chain Dynamics

**Lead time models**: EAF transformers 12-18+ months. Refractory bricks 4-12 weeks. Graphite electrodes 8-16 weeks. Scrap days to weeks. Ferro-alloys 4-8 weeks. Construction services 2-8 weeks mobilization. Commissioning specialists 6-12 months advance booking.

**Substitutability map**: transformer non-substitutable once ordered. Refractories partially substitutable (qualification testing needed). Electrodes substitutable between qualified suppliers (diameter/quality matching). Scrap grades partially (HMS-I ↔ shredded at chemistry cost). Ferro-alloys largely commodity. Services substitutable in theory, high relationship switching cost.

**Single-source risk register**: which items have only one qualified supplier? Which have components from only one country (EAF shell from China = live example)? Which services rely on specific named individuals? System maintains live register.

### Financial Modeling

**Total Cost of Ownership — Refractories**: unit price × quantity + reline labor + energy for new lining heating − value of longer campaign life (fewer relines = less downtime). Cheapest brick per unit might be most expensive per tonne of steel if campaign life is 30% shorter.

**Total Cost of Ownership — Scrap**: price/tonne at source + transport cost + quality premium/penalty (residual elements) + processing cost − energy benefit of cleaner scrap.

**Total Cost of Ownership — Services**: day rate × duration + mobilization/demobilization + risk cost of delay + rework cost − knowledge transfer value.

The system must calculate and present TCO, not just purchase price, for every significant procurement decision.

## 3.4 The Five-Layer Intelligence Model

### Layer definitions

1. **Data** (raw signals): order volumes, delivery dates, invoice amounts, quality results, scrap grades, price/tonne, tonnage received, emissions readings, refractory wear measurements, electrode consumption rates
2. **Insights** (patterns): supplier delivery reliability trends, price volatility indices, quality drift detection, consumption rate anomalies
3. **Diagnosis** (causes): root cause attribution, correlation between operational changes and performance shifts
4. **Predictions** (what will happen): forecast accuracy, confidence intervals, lead time for prediction
5. **Prescriptions** (what to do): specific recommended actions with reasoning, alternatives, and trade-offs

### Concrete examples — Scrap Steel

**Data**: HMS-I price £312/t (+12% 4wk). Volume received: 38,200t this month. Cu avg from Supplier C: 0.08% (spec <0.10%). Supplier A on-time: 94%.

**Insights**: price outpacing national average by 6 points. Supplier C Cu trending up 0.03% over 6 months — still within spec but approaching limit. Availability historically drops 8-12% in December-January.

**Diagnosis**: HMS-I price increase driven by aggressive Turkish buying — 3 major EAFs restocking post-maintenance. Supplier C Cu drift correlated with shift to end-of-life vehicle scrap (higher inherent Cu). Supplier B delivery reliability drop coincides with fleet changeover.

**Predictions**: Turkish buying pressure likely to ease in 3-4 weeks (Ramadan production patterns), confidence medium. If Supplier C Cu trend continues, spec breach within 4 months, probability 72%. Q3 spot requirement ~42,000t at projected prices = £1.2M budget exposure.

**Prescriptions**: defer spot scrap buy 3 weeks — save ~£8/t. Place Supplier C on quality watch, request meeting on source mix, begin Supplier D qualification if no reversal (8 weeks). Advance Q3 contract negotiation before July 1 tariffs — 3 pre-qualified suppliers ready for RFQ.

### Concrete examples — Refractories

**Data**: Zone 3 wear 0.42mm/heat. Campaign life: 720 heats (was 850). Unit cost £2,530/t (RHI baseline). Inventory covers next reline.

**Insights**: campaign life down 15% over 3 campaigns. Vesuvius XR-5 averages 920 heats vs. RHI 780 at 8% lower unit cost. Reline turnaround improved 15% with gunning technique. Refractory spend per tonne of steel 12% above industry median.

**Diagnosis**: Zone 3 decrease correlates with slag practice change in Campaign 47 — FeO increased 18%→24%, attacks MgO-C aggressively. Above-benchmark spend driven by taphole replacement every 3 heats (norm: 5) and ladle premium pricing. Taphole frequency linked to tapping temperature 15°C above target — operator practice, not refractory.

**Predictions**: at current Zone 3 wear rate, next reline June 15-22. If FeO not reduced, stabilizes ~700 heats (18% below historical). Electrode consumption trending 5% above forecast — safety stock breached in 6 weeks.

**Prescriptions**: Option A: revert slag practice to pre-Campaign-47 FeO levels — restores life, no cost change, may affect chemistry (engineering review needed). Option B: switch Zone 3 to Vesuvius XR-7 — 40% better FeO resistance, 2 reference sites report 30% longer life, 12% unit premium, net ROI positive after 2 campaigns. Recommend B as trial in Zone 3, maintain current in other zones as control. Also: reduce tapping temperature via operator retraining — taphole life 3→5 heats.

### Concrete examples — Services (Commissioning)

**Data**: 8/12 milestones on schedule, average variance +2.3 days. Variations 15% of contract value. Safety: 150,000 hours, zero lost-time incidents. Key commissioning engineer on-site 60% vs. 90% committed.

**Insights**: delays concentrated in electrical installation phase. Variations split 35% site conditions / 65% scope additions. Top-quartile safety performance. Key personnel substitution ongoing.

**Diagnosis**: electrical delays cascade from transformer shipping delay. Key engineer split — Contractor A won second EAF project in Germany, resource conflict. Scope additions driven by Tata engineering requests.

**Predictions**: final milestone ~18 days late — absorbed by transformer arrival (critical path constraint). Final outturn 18-22% above original value. If key engineer stays <70% on-site through hot commissioning, optimization period extends ~30% based on comparable projects.

**Prescriptions**: invoke contract clause 14.3 requiring 90% presence for named personnel — request remediation plan or contract adjustment. Establish change management protocol for future service procurements. No timeline risk currently — monitor transformer arrival weekly.

### How the frameworks connect

External factors create the context ("Red Sea shipping disruption"). Domain expertise interprets the impact ("this component is from China, no alternative source, Cape route adds 3 weeks, parallel construction absorbs 2 weeks"). Persona behavior determines communication ("CPD gets 30-second summary, HoP gets full timeline plus contingency, HoE gets integration milestone impact, CFO gets cost"). Five layers stack the intelligence (data: vessel position → insight: 3-week delay → diagnosis: Red Sea security → prediction: arrival June 15-22 → prescription: no action needed, flag next shipment).

This is one event. The experience center does this continuously across hundreds of procurement items simultaneously.

## 4.1 The Seven AI KPIs

### KPI 1: Proactive Alert Accuracy Rate

**Definition**: of all proactive alerts surfaced before the user asks, what percentage did the user act on, acknowledge as useful, or confirm as accurate?

**Why it matters**: the single most important KPI for the proactive-by-default mandate. Alert fatigue is the death of proactive intelligence.

**Positive signals**: user clicks through to investigate. User takes action directly (strongest). User acknowledges useful but already handling.

**Negative signals**: user dismisses as irrelevant or already known. Alert expires without interaction.

**Measurement**: track every alert generated. Capture interaction (click, act, acknowledge, dismiss, ignore). Calculate (acted + acknowledged) / total over rolling 30-day windows.

**Data source**: system telemetry — alert generation logs, user interaction logs, action completion logs.

**Design tension**: not all alerts are equal. A missed critical-path alert is far more damaging than a missed minor price movement. Track both raw rate and severity-weighted rate.

**Target**: >75% overall. >90% for high-severity (critical-path/safety). Year 1 start: >60% overall, >80% high-severity.

### KPI 2: Decision Time Reduction

**Definition**: how much faster is the cycle from "procurement need identified" to "decision made and PO issued" compared to baseline?

**Why it matters**: for routine reprocurement, faster = less inventory buffer = lower working capital. For crisis procurement, faster = less downtime.

**Cycle stages**: requirement logged → vendor identification → comparison/evaluation → approval workflow → PO generation.

**Measurement**: timestamp every workflow stage. Compare average cycle time per category with vs. without the system. Baseline from historical data.

**Data source**: system timestamps, ERP/procurement system PO dates, historical data.

**Design tension**: speed must not sacrifice quality. Must pair with outcome metrics. Different categories have fundamentally different cycle times — segment by category, don't average.

**Target**: 40-60% reduction for routine reprocurement. 20-30% for complex sourcing. No target for emergency — goal is pre-loaded alternatives.

### KPI 3: Cost Avoidance Captured

**Definition**: total financial value of cost savings the system identified AND the user acted on. Not theoretical.

**Why it matters**: the ROI metric. The CFO cares about this above all. Directly answers "is this system paying for itself?"

**What counts**: price arbitrage (market dip, opportunistic buy). Vendor switch to lower-cost equivalent. Campaign life improvement (fewer relines). Consolidated purchasing discounts. Timing optimization. Penalty avoidance (early risk mitigation).

**What does not count**: suggestions nobody followed. Cost reductions without system input. Savings that would have happened anyway.

**Measurement**: for each adopted recommendation, log baseline cost and actual cost. Difference × volume = saving. Aggregate quarterly.

**Design tension**: cost avoidance is gameable. Methodology must be agreed with CFO upfront. Use lower of (a) last purchase price or (b) budgeted price as baseline.

**Target**: must exceed system operating cost. If system costs £500K/year, capture >£1M.

### KPI 4: Supply Risk Prediction Rate

**Definition**: of supply disruptions that actually occurred, what percentage did the system predict in advance with enough lead time?

**Why it matters**: the "moment of happiness" KPI. The Port Talbot EAF shell shipping delay is the test case.

**Disruption types**: delivery delay beyond threshold (2 days scrap, 1 week refractories, 2 weeks equipment). Quality rejection. Vendor failure. Force majeure. Price shock.

**What counts as predicted**: alert generated BEFORE disruption. Enough lead time for mitigation. Correctly identified nature of risk.

**Measurement**: disruption log (post-hoc) cross-referenced against alert log (pre-hoc). (Disruptions with prior alert / total disruptions) × 100.

**Design tension**: tension between prediction rate and false positive rate. Use tiered approach: high-confidence alerts surface prominently, medium-confidence in watch list.

**Target**: >60% for high-impact (critical path or >£100K). >40% medium-impact. Year 1: >40% and >25% respectively.

### KPI 5: Vendor Performance Intelligence Utilization

**Definition**: when evaluating a vendor, what percentage of the time does the user access the AI-generated vendor performance summary?

**Why it matters**: measures whether the intelligence layer is trusted.

**Utilization signals**: opens summary during evaluation. References system data in recommendation. Shares view with colleague (stakes credibility on system).

**Non-utilization**: downloads raw data for own Excel analysis. Calls vendor directly. Asks colleague without checking system. Decides on gut/habit.

**Measurement**: track evaluation events. For each, track whether AI summary was accessed. (Accessed / total evaluations) × 100.

**Design tension**: high utilization doesn't mean high trust — users might look and ignore. Ideally pair with periodic survey. <30% should trigger investigation.

**Target**: >70% for major evaluations (>£50K). >50% for routine. Year 1: >50% by month 6, >70% by month 12.

### KPI 6: Prescription Adoption Rate

**Definition**: when the system recommends a specific action, how often does the user follow it?

**Why it matters**: the ultimate trust metric. Low adoption = prescriptions wrong, trust absent, or missing context.

**Adopted**: explicitly accepts. Modifies slightly but follows essential direction.

**Rejected (must capture reason)**: already aware/handling. Disagree with analysis. Missing context system didn't have. Political/relationship reasons. Timing not right. Risk too high.

**Measurement**: track every prescription. Capture response + reason for rejections. (Adopted + modified) / total × 100.

**Design tension**: 100% adoption is a bad sign — means system only recommends obvious things. Segment by stakes: low (routine reorders), medium (vendor switches), high (primary vendor changes on critical path). Rejection feedback loop is critical — trains system and builds trust.

**Target**: >60% low-stakes. >40% medium. >25% high. Year 1: >50%, >30%, >20%.

### KPI 7: Cross-Package Dependency Detection

**Definition**: number of risks spanning multiple procurement packages — invisible to any single officer — that the system identified.

**Why it matters**: system-level intelligence no human can maintain. Hundreds of parallel packages. Refractory delay + electrode delay = commissioning timeline risk. Most aligned with Chief Project Director persona.

**Dependency types**: timing (Package A delay affects Package B start). Resource (competing for same crane/specialist). Budget (Package A overrun reduces Package B contingency). Quality (Package A quality affects Package B performance). Supply chain (shared supplier or route).

**Value metrics**: total dependencies detected. % validated as real by project team. % previously unknown (the true value measure).

**Measurement**: count unique dependencies surfaced. Validate with team. Track validated rate and previously-unknown rate.

**Design tension**: harder to set absolute target. Dependencies already known aren't adding intelligence.

**Target**: >50% validated as real. >30% previously unknown. 5-15 meaningful dependencies per month during active construction.

## 4.2 Trust Architecture

Four pillars running through everything — not a feature in one screen, but a layer across the entire experience.

### Explainability

Every recommendation shows reasoning. Not "switch to Vendor B" but "switch to Vendor B because: 94% delivery reliability vs. 78%, 22% longer campaign life at 8% higher unit cost, net ROI positive." Source logos, data lineage, confidence levels as trust anchors.

### Traceability

Every data point traces to its source system and timestamp. Steel procurement decisions are auditable. Audit trail native.

### Correctability

Users can override, and the system learns from it. Six rejection reason categories: already aware/handling, disagree with analysis, missing context, political/relationship, timing not right, risk too high. A procurement officer who overrides and is proven right must see the system adapt.

### Progressive disclosure

Right detail for right persona. CFO sees financial summary. Head of Engineering sees technical depth. Same underlying data, different presentation. Information density matched to decision authority.

## 4.4 The Five Fluid States

Single canvas with fluid states — not multiple pages or screens.

**State 1 — Entry / Intelligence Summary (proactive)**: first thing user sees. Not a dashboard of charts. A narrative intelligence summary: what's on track, what's at risk, what needs attention. Personalized per persona. "Moment of clarity" happens here.

**State 2 — Package Deep Dive (reactive)**: user drills into a specific procurement package. Full context: vendor comparisons for products, team/safety/references for services. Five-layer intelligence visible. "Moment of decision" happens here.

**State 3 — Comparison / Evaluation (active)**: the primary user action. Multi-axis vendor comparison with recommendation highlighted but not forced. Historical performance, price, quality, delivery, sustainability, risk. Trust architecture visible — reasoning, confidence, sources.

**State 4 — Alert / Prescription Response (reactive-proactive)**: responding to a proactive alert or prescription. System shows "how it identified this" — which layer, which data. Accept, modify, or override with reason capture. Trust built or broken here.

**State 5 — Reporting / Audit (extractive)**: pulling data out for external use. Board reports, government submissions, sustainability reports, financial reviews. Audit-ready exports per persona's reporting needs.

## 4.6 Design Principles

The experience center is proactive by default, reactive as secondary. The entry experience is proactive — what matters surfaces before the user asks. The reactive layer is what they drill into.

Product and service procurement must be handled with different UI, different data, different trust signals. A product package shows spec compliance, price/quality/delivery comparison, consumption rates, campaign life. A service package shows team CVs, named personnel presence, safety record, past project references.

Scrap steel supply is the highest-priority domain and must be visually elevated — not equal to lower-priority packages.

The five intelligence layers must be visible throughout — not hidden behind a secondary view. Every package, every alert, every prescription should show which layer it comes from and how the system arrived there.

External factors (regulatory countdowns, geopolitical signals, market data) must be accessible from any state — not buried in a settings page.

Cross-package dependencies must be visible to the Chief Project Director — this is the system-level intelligence that justifies the platform's existence.

Trust architecture runs through everything. Every AI-generated element — narratives, metrics, alerts, prescriptions — must show its reasoning, data source, and confidence level. The system must accept overrides, capture the reason, and learn from them.

The language, terminology, and environment must be manufacturing-native — not generic enterprise software.

99% ideation, 1% execution. Paper first. No screens until the team can explain the domain, the personas, the frameworks, and the primary user action without hesitation.
